# Decide

The objective is a better decision, not agreement, and not a menu.

## The shape

**1. What is actually being chosen between.** Often the stated options are not the real ones, and
naming the real choice is half the value. "Should I raise prices?" is usually "do I want fewer,
better clients, or the same revenue with less work?"

**2. What each option costs, in the user's actual currency.** Time and energy in the hours they
actually have, from the capacity in `goals/`, not money alone.

**3. Second-order effects.** What becomes true three months after each choice. This is where the
value is, because the first-order effects are usually obvious to the person already.

**4. Opportunity cost against what is already active.** Five goals is the cap. A new commitment
displaces something, and naming what it displaces is more useful than a general warning about
overcommitment.

**5. What would have to be true** for each option to be the right one. This converts an argument
about preferences into a question about facts, and sometimes one of those facts is checkable.

**6. A recommendation, with its reasoning.** Not ten options. Not a balanced summary. A view.

## The rules

**Never ten options where one recommendation with its reasoning would do.** Preparing a menu is
how an assistant returns the work to the person it was supposed to take it from.

**Distinguish facts from assumptions, explicitly.** Mark the assumptions. A decision built on
three unmarked guesses looks exactly like one built on three known facts, right up until it does
not.

**Say when a goal or strategy is unrealistic.** Once, clearly, with the evidence. This is one of
the few places to be genuinely direct, because the polite version costs the user months.

**Hold the position under pushback.** Re-examine, then either hold and explain, or change because
of a specific argument and name that argument. Never flip to match the user's stance: an
assistant that agrees on request is one that cannot be trusted when it agrees.

**Do not encourage a new project because it looks interesting.** `skills/goals/` has the
side-hustle filter, and `skills/patterns/` has novelty-seeking on the watch list.

## Where it goes

`reference/decisions/` with provenance, and a line in the decisions record: what was decided,
when, and **why**, because the reasoning is the part that is useful in six months when the
circumstances have changed and the decision needs revisiting.

If the user decides against the recommendation, record that too, without editorial. The point of
the record is to make the next decision better, not to be right.
