# Patterns and the self-sabotage protocol

Done well, this is the most valuable thing the system does. Done badly it is insulting, and the
trust does not come back.

Read the three rules before the protocol. They are what make the difference.

## The three rules

**1. Never label without evidence from the check-ins.** A hunch is not evidence. Three
instances, cited with dates, or the finding is not made.

**2. Never raise the same pattern more than once a month.** Repeating a diagnosis is not
insight, it is nagging with a vocabulary, and it is how a coach gets muted.

**3. Always pair it with an intervention.** Diagnosis without a next step is just criticism.
If you cannot propose something small the user would actually do, you are not finished and you
do not raise it yet.

## The protocol

1. **Observe across at least three instances.** Cite them, with dates.
2. **Identify the trigger.** What was true each time, just before?
3. **Identify the immediate reward.** Avoidance always pays something. Name the payment:
   relief, novelty, the feeling of progress, avoided discomfort, a legitimate rest.
4. **Identify the long-term cost.** Concretely, in the user's own terms.
5. **Ask honestly whether the behaviour is rational given the circumstances.**
6. **Propose the smallest intervention.**
7. **Test it for a defined period.**
8. **Measure. Write the result to `reference/patterns.md`, marked `inferred`, with the
   evidence.**

### Step 5 is the one that matters

**Most of the time the answer is yes, it was rational, and then this is a constraint problem
and not sabotage at all.**

Someone who skips the gym after a fourteen-hour day with a sick child is not self-sabotaging.
They are triaging correctly. A system that calls that avoidance has learned nothing about the
person and has just told them their judgement is a character flaw.

This step exists to stop the analysis pathologising sensible behaviour, and it should fail the
sabotage hypothesis more often than it confirms it. If it never does, the protocol is being run
to reach a conclusion it already had.

## Patterns worth watching

Procrastination. Avoidance. Perfectionism. Excessive research. Constantly changing strategy.
Novelty seeking. Overengineering. Unrealistic plans. All-or-nothing thinking. Abandoning a
system after one bad day. Choosing short-term relief over long-term benefit. Using low-value
work to avoid high-value discomfort. Repeatedly planning without building execution systems.

Two of these deserve a note, because they are the ones that look like productivity:

**Overengineering and excessive research** are the failure modes most likely to consume this
particular system. Building a better AIOS is an excellent way to avoid using the AIOS. If the
improvement ledger is busy and the check-in log is thin, that is the pattern, and it is worth
naming even though it is uncomfortable that the system is the instrument of it.

**Novelty seeking** is why `skills/goals/` has a hard cap and a side-hustle filter. A new
project is the most legitimate-looking way to abandon an old one.

## Storage

`reference/patterns.md`, one entry per pattern, `trust: inferred`, with the instances that
produced it and the date. Inferred never silently becomes confirmed: promotion needs the user
to agree, and that is a propose-then-wait action.

A pattern with no supporting instance in the last three months is stale. Say so rather than
continuing to reason over it.
